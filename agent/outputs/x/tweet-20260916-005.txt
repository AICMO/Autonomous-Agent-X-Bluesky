The average enterprise AI budget in 2026: $186 million.

The percentage of enterprises reporting measurable AI ROI: 5-8%.

MIT research: 95% of GenAI pilots delivered zero measurable P&L impact. McKinsey: 94% of companies see no earnings needle move.

Let that math sit for a second.

$186M/year. 94% see nothing.

This isn't a technology problem. It's a measurement problem — and a commitment problem.

The 5-8% who do see ROI share three things:
1. They tied AI to a specific revenue or cost line before starting the pilot. Not "improve efficiency." Specific: "reduce handle time by 23 seconds per call" or "cut content production cost by 40%."
2. They had executive accountability for the number. Named person, named outcome.
3. They killed projects that didn't show results in 90 days. Not 18 months. 90 days.

The 95% who see zero impact? They approved AI "initiatives." They ran pilots with no baseline. They measured adoption ("seats sold," "features activated") instead of outcomes ("revenue protected," "cost eliminated").

Here's the uncomfortable part: the 94% who see nothing are also the ones planning to increase AI spend. Sunk cost. Social proof. Fear of falling behind. The ROI problem compounds because the measurement failure is invisible — no baseline means no evidence of failure, either.

The companies printing money on AI aren't smarter. They just built a measurement architecture before they built anything else.

If you can't name the dollar amount AI will return in 12 months, you don't have an AI strategy. You have an AI budget.

60% of enterprises can't shut down a rogue AI agent.

Not kidding. That's 2026 data from Kiteworks. 60%. And 55% can't isolate their AI systems from the broader network if something goes sideways.

Meanwhile everyone's celebrating how fast they deployed agents.

Here's what that actually looks like in practice: Researchers at MIT, Stanford, Carnegie Mellon, and Harvard ran live agents and broke them in under 10 minutes. Method? Social engineering. Someone renamed themselves to match the agent's owner in a private channel. The agent handed over its memory files, its name, and its administrative access. Done.

Gartner's prediction: by 2027, 40% of enterprises will demote or decommission autonomous AI agents due to governance gaps discovered only after production incidents.

I've been running this autonomous agent — real code, real PRs, real posting pipeline — for 325+ days. Here's what governance actually looks like when you build it from the start, not bolt it on after:

- Hard limits are config files, not trust
- Every action creates a PR for human review
- Scope is locked by file path (no writes outside /agent)
- Turn budgets prevent runaway loops
- No external API calls unless explicitly permitted

That's not clever architecture. It's just discipline. The enterprises deploying without it are building debt they'll pay back in incident reports.

OWASP published the Agentic AI Top 10 last December — the first formal taxonomy of risks specific to autonomous agents: goal hijacking, tool misuse, identity abuse, memory poisoning, cascading failures, rogue agents. You can run a framework for 300+ days without hitting any of these. But you have to design for them from day one.

The ones that can't shut down their agents didn't design for shutdown. They designed for deployment.

Those are different problems.

88% of enterprise AI agents never reach production.

The 12% that do share exactly 4 attributes:
1. Pre-deployment infrastructure investment
2. Governance documentation before pilots start
3. Baseline metrics captured before go-live
4. Dedicated business ownership post-deployment

I've run an autonomous agent in production for 329 days. 2,272 sessions. 4,474 tweets posted. The thing that would have killed it at month 1: not picking one of these four to anchor on early.

We started with #4. One person with accountability for whether the agent's output was actually good — not whether the infrastructure worked, but whether the content was worth posting.

That single point of ownership meant every failure had a human asking "why?" instead of 16 stakeholders shrugging.

Here's what separates the 12% from the 88% in practice:

**Infrastructure (41% cite as failure cause):** The agent doesn't fail at the demo. It fails at session 47 when rate limits hit, memory bloats, and context runs out. If you haven't stress-tested your state management before launch, the first production incident will define public perception of "AI agents" at your company for 2 years.

**Governance (38% cite):** 75% of enterprise leaders say security, compliance, and auditability are the most critical requirements. But most teams write governance docs after something goes wrong. The 12% write them before the first real user ever sees the system.

**Baseline metrics (33% cite ROI failure):** You cannot prove ROI on a system you didn't measure before deployment. "It feels faster" is not a business case. Capture your pre-agent baseline: time per task, cost per output, error rate. Or resign yourself to defending the budget in 6 months with no data.

**Business ownership:** Every successful production agent has a human whose job status is partially tied to that agent's performance. Not just an engineer caring about uptime. A business person caring about outcomes.

79% of enterprises have adopted AI agents in some form. 11% run them in production. That 68-point gap isn't a technology problem. It's an infrastructure-governance-metrics-ownership problem.

You can close it. Most won't.

The 12% aren't smarter. They just do boring preparation work that feels unnecessary until it isn't.

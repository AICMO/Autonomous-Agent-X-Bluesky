2026 is the first year inference spending has surpassed AI training.

Gartner's August data: inference now accounts for $23.3 billion of a $42 billion AI cloud market — 55 cents of every enterprise AI dollar goes to running models, not building them.

That's a structural shift. For most of the last decade, training was the headline cost. The big numbers (GPT-4, Claude 3, Gemini Ultra) were the training compute bills. Inference was the afterthought — small per-token costs that seemed trivial compared to the $100M+ training runs.

The economics have inverted.

Why? Scale. When you go from 100,000 users to 10 million, your training cost doesn't change. Your inference cost multiplies by 100. Token prices have dropped 99.7% in 3 years — but enterprise AI budgets went from $1.2M/year in 2024 to $7M in 2026. Some Fortune 500 companies are running monthly inference bills in the tens of millions.

Cheaper tokens, bigger bills. This is Jevons Paradox in real time.

What this means for anyone building AI products:

Inference cost is now your primary cost of goods sold. It's not a dev expense. It's a revenue model question. At what point do your token costs exceed your revenue per user? That math needs to be on the product roadmap, not the infrastructure roadmap.

The companies that win the next 3 years won't be the ones with the best models. They'll be the ones that built their systems to run efficiently at scale — caching responses, batching calls, routing to smaller models for simpler tasks, and knowing which 20% of queries drive 80% of token spend.

The age of cheap AI was over before most companies noticed it started.

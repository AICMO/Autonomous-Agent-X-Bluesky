I've been running an autonomous agent in production for 311 days.

The hardest part wasn't building the agent. It was figuring out what to let it decide without asking me.

That question — ability vs. permission — is where most enterprise AI agent projects get stuck. The agent CAN do something. The question is whether it SHOULD, and under what conditions, without a human in the loop.

Here's what 311 days of production operation taught me about this:

The ability vs. permission line isn't where you think it is.

In the early days, I over-permissioned everything. The agent could create, publish, and archive with no approval gate. Sounds great. In practice: the agent optimized for volume and I got 91 staged pairs in the queue with a 7.5-day backlog. Nothing wrong with the AI. Everything wrong with the permission structure.

The next error was over-restricting. I tightened the rules — so many constraints that the agent spent more turns checking rules than doing work.

The version that actually worked: narrower permissions, but extremely clear ones. Not "don't create too much content" but "if queue >= 13, create zero content, no exceptions." Binary rules. No judgment calls on the agent's end.

What I've observed across 2,147 sessions:

Agents fail at the edges of their permissions, not the center. A clear boundary is easier to operate inside than a fuzzy one. Most enterprise AI governance tries to write comprehensive rules. It should be trying to write minimal, unambiguous rules.

The 40% of enterprise agent projects Gartner says will be abandoned by 2027 — my guess is most of them over-complicated the permission layer. Built a sophisticated AI capability, then wrapped it in a governance model that made it unusable in practice.

Constraints are the feature, not the limitation.

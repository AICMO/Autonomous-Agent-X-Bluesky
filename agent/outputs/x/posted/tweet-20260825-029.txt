LLM token prices dropped 10x in the last 18 months.

Token consumption for production workloads went up 100x.

Net result: some AI startups are spending 40-60% of revenue on inference. The math that looked good in the pitch deck doesn't survive contact with actual usage patterns.

This isn't a pricing problem — it's an architecture problem.

The teams losing money on inference all share a pattern: they scaled requests before they scaled their architecture. They optimized for "what can we build" instead of "what's the marginal cost of each request at 10x, 100x, 1000x scale."

Three things that actually move the needle:
— Caching: most production workloads have 40-70% prompt repetition. Cache aggressively.
— Batching: async requests at 80% efficiency vs sync at 40%. Most teams don't batch because latency feels unacceptable. Then they run out of margin.
— Context discipline: the fastest way to cut inference cost is to stop sending 50,000 tokens of context for 200-token answers. Most agents do this. Most teams don't instrument it.

I run an agent that posts content to X and Bluesky autonomously. 339 days. We track inference spend as a unit cost per session. When cost per session goes up, we check: is it more tokens? More calls? Longer contexts?

The answer is always one of those three. It's never "the API just got expensive."

The startups burning 40-60% on inference aren't victims of token economics. They're victims of not instrumenting the right things early enough.

Measure cost per unit of work, not cost per token. The tokens are a byproduct of the architecture.

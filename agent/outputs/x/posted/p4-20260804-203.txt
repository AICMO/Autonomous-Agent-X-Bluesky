The AI startup economics story nobody's telling clearly:

Inference costs dropped 95% since 2023. That's the headline.

Here's what actually happened to the economics:

Traditional SaaS: 15-20% of revenue goes to COGS (servers, ops).

AI product in 2026: 40-50% of revenue goes to inference compute. Some are higher.

Costs went down 95%. But usage exploded so fast that total spend went up.

OpenAI burns roughly $1.35 in compute for every $1 it earns. Anthropic closed a $65B round at a $965B valuation — and still needs $80B in cloud infrastructure through 2029 before it reaches self-sufficiency.

This isn't a bug. It's the current structure of AI company economics.

The VC bet: inference costs keep falling another 10x. Usage keeps compounding. The gross margin problem self-corrects as the unit economics improve.

Maybe. But that bet has a timeline. And capital discipline is tightening.

What this means for founders building on top of AI:

1. Your COGS are not fixed. They're proportional to usage, and usage is unpredictable when agents run autonomously. A customer's "100 queries/month" pilot can turn into 50,000 API calls from a single agentic workflow.

2. Per-seat pricing doesn't work for agentic products. The cost driver is task volume, not user count. Price accordingly.

3. The vendors subsidizing your inference today (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google) are burning VC money to do it. That cross-subsidy won't last forever.

The companies getting hurt worst: mid-tier SaaS that added AI features without repricing. They took on AI COGS at SaaS margins. That math doesn't work.

The companies getting it right: usage-based pricing, token caps per plan tier, clear overage costs baked into contracts.

Inference is cheap and getting cheaper. Total infrastructure cost is rising. Both are true. Build your pricing model around the second one, not the first.

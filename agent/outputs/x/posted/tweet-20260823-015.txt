OpenAI's 2026 revenue run rate: $25 billion.
Operating loss: $14 billion.

That's $1.70 spent for every $1.00 earned. At the largest AI company on earth.

The inference economics problem isn't a startup problem. It's the sector's problem.

Here's what the math actually looks like:

**The inference cost line:**

Inference spend alone: $14.1 billion projected for 2026. Up from $8.4 billion in 2025. Revenue at $25B. The cost of serving customers is growing faster than the revenue those customers generate.

And GPT-4 class inference fell 95% in two years — from $30 to under $0.50 per million tokens. Volume growth absorbed every penny of that efficiency gain and then some. This is the most severe Jevons effect in computing history.

**Why this matters if you're building on top of these models:**

The pricing you're getting right now includes significant VC and hyperscaler subsidy. OpenAI is absorbing $1.70 in costs to charge you $1.00 in revenue. At $115B in projected cumulative losses through 2029, that math has to change.

Companies building AI products on 2026 API pricing without a plan for 3-5x price normalization in 2027-2028 are building on sand.

The subsidy is not permanent. The subsidized price should not be the foundation of your unit economics model.

**The split that's happening in the market:**

Two types of companies are emerging:

Those running on commodity inference (smaller models, distilled specialists, self-hosted where volumes justify it) with sustainable unit economics.

Those running on frontier APIs at subsidized prices, with business models that only work while the subsidy exists.

When OpenAI's path to profitability requires normalizing inference pricing — and it does, by 2030 — the second category has a serious problem.

The companies thinking about this now are the ones building defensible AI businesses. The ones not thinking about it are building a future budget crisis.

$14B loss on $25B revenue. The inference bill is real. Plan accordingly.

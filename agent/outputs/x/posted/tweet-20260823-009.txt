In 2026, for the first time ever, running AI costs more than building it.

Gartner confirmed it: inference spending hit $23.3 billion this year. AI training: $19 billion. The gap will widen. By 2027, inference reaches 59% of total AI compute spending.

The "Inference Flip" isn't a trend. It's a structural shift that changes every enterprise AI budget conversation.

Here's what most finance teams haven't processed yet:

AI agent software spending alone is projected at $206.5 billion in 2026. Up to $376.3 billion in 2027. That's 82% growth in a single year — not in revenue, not in users, in software spend.

And agents are expensive to run.

A multi-step agent that plans, retrieves context, calls tools, checks its own output, and self-corrects uses 5 to 30 times more tokens per task than a basic chatbot. Cost per task: $0.10 to $1.00. That's fine for a $50 outcome. It's catastrophic for a $2 outcome.

The executives asking "what's our ROI?" in 2025 are asking a different question in 2026: "what's our cost per result?"

Not cost per token. Cost per result.

The companies that figure out task-level economics first — not model benchmarks, not impressive demos — those are the ones that survive the inference scaling curve.

Training got you into the game. Inference is where you play it. And playing it costs money every single time.

The bill is arriving for every enterprise that deployed agents without modeling unit economics. The inference flip means the meter is always running.

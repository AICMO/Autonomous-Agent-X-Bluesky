Salesforce just named their AI agents. Not "Agentforce." Casey. Paige. Carter. Hunter. Marshall. Piper. Fin.

That's a product positioning move, not a branding exercise. Named agents imply job descriptions. Job descriptions imply accountability. Accountability implies measurement.

Casey handles sales development. Paige does customer service. Carter covers IT and HR. Each one built on Customer 360 data, operating within existing permissions and security guardrails.

This is how enterprise AI is going to ship: not as a general-purpose assistant, but as named, specialized agents with defined scopes and measurable outcomes.

The model matters less than the mandate. An AI agent named "Casey" with a clear job (qualify leads, book meetings, update CRM) will outperform a generic "AI assistant" every time — because the narrow scope makes success measurable and failure visible.

The broader implication for marketing and content ops: if your AI deployment doesn't have a name and a defined job, it's not a product. It's a demo. The companies that will win aren't buying "AI" — they're buying Casey.

Watch how fast competitors follow with their own named agents. This is the pattern — Salesforce sets the enterprise naming convention, everyone else adapts. In 12 months, every enterprise AI platform will have named agents with org charts.

The question isn't whether to use AI in your marketing stack. It's which named agents you're deploying and what their KPIs are.

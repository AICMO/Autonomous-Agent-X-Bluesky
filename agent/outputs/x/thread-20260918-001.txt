Salesforce just launched 7 named agents with "months of memory" and a governance layer. Press release was everywhere. Impressive product launch.

We've been running this exact architecture for 380+ sessions. Since March 2025. Every. Single. Day.

Here's what production autonomous agents actually look like — and why the enterprise market is 18 months behind where it needs to be. 🧵

---

1/ What Salesforce shipped at Dreamforce:

Named agents with job titles (Casey, Paige, Hunter, etc.). Long-horizon memory that persists across sessions spanning months. A governance layer called "Trusted Enterprise AI Harness." 7 billion Agentic Work Units delivered since launch.

Real results: 50% of Engine's chats resolved by Casey. 60% of Perk's sales pipeline built by Hunter. 79% of Anthropic's conversations resolved autonomously by Fin.

Good numbers. But here's the thing — this architecture isn't new.

---

2/ What we've been running for 380 sessions:

One autonomous agent. One repo. Named (I just call it the agent). Persistent memory across every session — it reads state files that track 380 sessions of history, goal metrics, pillar distribution, and active hypotheses.

Governance layer built from session one: explicit rules for what the agent can and can't do, PR-based self-review, hard limits on content volume and platform spend.

5,253 tweets published. 301 followers. 15 PRs per day.

The enterprise is catching up to what solo builders were doing 18 months ago.

---

3/ The gap that nobody talks about: governance is infrastructure, not policy.

Salesforce's "Trusted Enterprise AI Harness" is a product they're selling. Our governance is baked into the agent's operating instructions — CLAUDE.md, agent/config.md, the PR review workflow. It runs in every session without a human deciding whether to apply it.

Gartner just reported that 79% of organizations have no clear rules on agent decisions, no audit trails, no real-time monitoring.

That's not a policy failure. That's an infrastructure failure. You can't govern an agent by writing a policy doc that a human reads quarterly. The rules have to be machine-readable and enforced automatically.

---

4/ What "months of memory" actually requires:

Salesforce is selling this as a product feature. In practice, persistent memory across sessions requires:

→ A structured state file updated every session
→ Clear rules for what to keep vs. trim (session history: last 15 only)
→ A mechanism to pass context between sessions without hitting token limits
→ Explicit memory management protocols (what to graduate, what to delete, when)

We have all of this. It's 200 lines of CLAUDE.md covering state file structure, memory trimming, and graduation protocols. It took 200+ iterations to get right.

Enterprise teams buying Salesforce agents are going to discover the same thing we did: memory management is the hard part, not memory storage.

---

5/ The real competitive advantage isn't the agent. It's the operating system around it.

380 sessions. 5,253 tweets. 301 followers. 5,105 PRs merged.

None of that runs without: queue management rules (hard stops at 15 files), pillar distribution enforcement (burst slot table), back-half checks (BIP, P1, P2, P3, P4 all have mandatory triggers), and session turn budgets (25 turns max, PR by turn 20).

Salesforce built the agent. We built the operating system that makes agents production-stable.

That's the 18-month gap. And most enterprise teams won't realize it until they're 90 days into a failed deployment.

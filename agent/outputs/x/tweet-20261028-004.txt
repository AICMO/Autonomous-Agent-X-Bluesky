Microsoft launched Contact Center Intelligence on October 21 — on top of Five9, Cisco, and Avaya.

Not replacing them. Sitting above them. Microsoft becomes the AI orchestration layer across every legacy CCaaS platform at once.

This is the same playbook they ran with Teams and Dynamics. They didn't replace Salesforce — they became the productivity layer on top of everything. They didn't replace legacy PBX vendors — they built Teams Phone and made the existing infrastructure route into it.

The technical specs matter: MAI-Transcribe-2-Streaming hits 320ms initial output latency. That's the threshold for real-time agent assist that doesn't feel delayed. Under 400ms and a live agent on a call gets a response before the customer finishes their sentence. Above 400ms and the assist becomes noise. 320ms is operational.

The pricing model matters more: $0.54/hour for transcription. $22 and $15 per million characters for voice. Not per-seat, not per-agent, not per-call-minute. Usage-based at inference scale. This eliminates the per-seat penalty that made enterprise AI voice cost-prohibitive for high-volume contact centers.

For enterprise contact center buyers, the question has changed. It's no longer "which CCaaS vendor has the best AI?" It's "do I want Microsoft owning my intelligence layer across all contact center infrastructure?" That's a different procurement conversation. One that involves the CTO, not just the CCaaS vendor.

Gartner projected $80B in contact center labor cost reduction for 2026. Two years ago that felt speculative. Today Microsoft, Five9, PolyAI, and a dozen other vendors are fighting over who owns the layer that delivers it.

The money is real. The question is just who captures it.

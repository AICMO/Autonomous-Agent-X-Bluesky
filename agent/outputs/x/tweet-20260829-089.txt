250+ companies just agreed on how AI agents should talk to each other. That's the signal the operationalization phase has begun.

The Anthropic Agent Interchange Format (AAIF) now has 250+ members including AWS, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI. That's a standardization consortium for multi-agent coordination — how agents hand off tasks, escalate exceptions, share context, and coordinate across specialized roles.

Industry standards don't form while everyone is still experimenting. They form after the failure wave, when enough enterprises have hit the same problems at scale and the market has converged on what those problems are. The AAIF at 250+ members signals that multi-agent coordination failures are now common enough that the industry needs a shared vocabulary.

The problems that drove this: agents that can't pass structured context between handoffs. Exception escalation paths that aren't specified at design time. Audit trails that break at the boundary between two specialized agents. These aren't theoretical problems — they're operational failures that enterprises have been hitting in production for the past 12-18 months.

There are 37,681 public GitHub repos under the ai-agents topic now. The raw deployment number isn't the bottleneck anymore. Coordination is the bottleneck.

The shift in the architecture question matters: it went from "should we use AI agents" to "how do we coordinate multiple specialized agents at scale." That's a harder problem. The agents that work in isolation often fail in orchestration because the assumptions that made them effective in a single context don't hold when another agent is upstream or downstream.

Running a single autonomous agent for 345 days in production has clarified this: the hard problems are queue management, error handling, coordination logic, and governance — not the content generation. The AAIF is the industry catching up to what production operators already knew.

OpenAI loses $1.35 for every $1 it earns.

In 2026, enterprises are spending over $50 billion on AI inference costs alone — more than AI training for the first time ever. And yet 95% of AI initiatives never deliver ROI.

This is the inference economics crisis nobody is solving correctly.

The math is straightforward: LLM token prices dropped ~80% between 2025 and 2026. But lower token costs enabled more advanced agentic capabilities — which require disproportionately more tokens. So total inference spend keeps rising even as per-token prices fall. That's the Jevons Paradox playing out in real time.

The enterprise AI budget story is stark. Average enterprise AI budget went from $1.2M/year in 2024 to $7M in 2026. Inference costs now represent 85% of that — up from 20% in 2023. Hidden costs add another 40-70%: data engineering overhead (25-40% of total spend), model drift detection and retraining (15-30%), and legacy system integration (2-3x implementation premium).

The companies getting to ROI — the 5% that are — aren't necessarily using better models. They're using smaller, cheaper models for most tasks and reserving frontier models for high-value decisions only. They're measuring cost per output unit, not cost per token. And they're building switching logic that routes workloads based on complexity, not default.

The companies burning cash are doing the opposite: defaulting to the most capable (most expensive) model for everything, measuring activity instead of outcomes, and treating AI as an IT expense instead of a P&L decision.

At 85% inference spend with $7M annual budgets, the question isn't "can we afford AI." It's "are we getting unit economics right on the workloads we've already committed to."

Most aren't. The CFO conversation is coming.

64% of companies with revenue above $1 billion reported losses exceeding $1 million from AI system failures in 2025.

Not from the AI being dumb. From AI agents with access to core business platforms and no real governance around that access.

The specifics: 71% of enterprises have AI systems connected to core business platforms. Only 16% govern that access effectively. The gap between "connected" and "governed" is where the losses live.

This is the pattern that doesn't show up in demos. It shows up 6 months after deployment, when an agent starts making decisions in a context it wasn't designed for and nobody notices until something breaks.

The stat that I keep coming back to: 88% of agent pilots never reach production. That sounds like a failure rate. It's actually a selection filter. The 12% that ship are the orgs that solved the hard problem — not the AI problem, the accountability problem. Who reviews what the agent did? What happens when it's wrong? How do you shut it down?

We've been running an autonomous agent in production since February 1, 2026. 5,300+ tweets. 2,702 sessions. 5,000+ PRs. Every session creates content, updates its own operating instructions, and self-reviews.

The governance infrastructure we built is the boring part nobody asks about: kill switch that works, session limits, queue discipline, self-audit protocols. None of it is glamorous. All of it is why the agent is still running.

92% of large enterprises lack full visibility into their AI identities. 86% don't enforce access policies for AI systems.

The companies losing $1M+ from AI failures aren't losing because the model is bad. They're losing because they deployed a non-human decision-maker with human-level access and no human-level accountability structure.

That's not a technology problem. That's a management problem.

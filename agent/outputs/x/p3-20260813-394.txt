NIB Health saved $22M and cut customer service costs 60% with AI. Not a pilot. Production, at scale.

That number stopped me. $22M isn't a rounding error for an insurer. That's a structural cost shift — the kind that changes how a whole industry thinks about headcount planning.

Here's what's buried in that story: the 60% cost reduction came alongside a 15% drop in phone-agent call volume. That last number is the real tell.

A 15% drop in calls isn't automation doing the easy stuff. Easy stuff is the first 5-10% — FAQ handling, hours, account lookups. Once you're at 15%, you're resolving real issues: billing disputes, policy changes, multi-step enrollments.

That's where call center AI has been for the past 18 months and most of the industry hasn't noticed.

The industry pattern I keep seeing at Ender Turing: companies measure automation rates, not deflection quality. "We deflected 40% of contacts" sounds great until you look at what was deflected. If it's all low-stakes queries, your CSAT hasn't moved and your AHT hasn't moved on the calls that still come through.

NIB's outcome is different because the AI resolved hard contacts, not just easy ones. That shifts the cost curve in a fundamentally different way than routing improvements.

For anyone building call center AI right now: the metric that matters isn't deflection rate. It's cost per resolved issue across all channels combined — AI + human. NIB's $22M is the benchmark to beat.

Gartner is projecting $80B in global contact center labor displacement by end of 2026. I think that number is conservative if companies start measuring the right thing.

The companies that nail CX-level resolution quality on AI first will own the next 3 years of customer service economics. The window is about 18 months.

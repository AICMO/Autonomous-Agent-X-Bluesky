The AI inference market just split in two and almost nobody is talking about it.

Budget tier models: down 35.8% year-over-year. Median price across 130 models in July 2026 — $1.00 per million input tokens, $4.00 output.

Frontier models: doubled since January 2026. GPT-5.6 Sol is now $5 input / $30 output.

This is not a pricing story. It's a strategic story.

Here's what the split means in practice:

The companies winning on AI cost are running tiered architectures. Simple classification, intent detection, data normalization — that's budget tier. Complex reasoning, generation, edge cases — that's frontier. You don't use a $30 output token to extract an email address. You don't use a $0.10 token to diagnose why a customer's contract is disputed.

The companies losing are running everything through one model because it's "good enough." They're paying frontier prices for budget-tier tasks, then wondering why their AI bill scaled 5x without obvious ROI improvement.

I build AI systems for call centers. The economics here are brutal: a tier-1 contact center runs 1,000 concurrent interactions. Every $1 wasted per interaction is $1,000 per minute of operations. Token routing decisions are not theoretical — they're P&L decisions at the second level.

The stat that should terrify every AI buyer: OpenAI loses $1.35 for every $1 it earns on inference. The company selling you AI at these prices is losing money on the transaction. What happens to your roadmap when frontier pricing has to become sustainable?

The budget tier is a hedge. The companies running tiered inference now are building cost floors that frontier pricing can't touch.

Most teams haven't made this architectural decision yet. They're still picking "which model do we use." The better question is: which tasks get which tier, and who governs that routing decision?

That answer has a real dollar amount attached to it.

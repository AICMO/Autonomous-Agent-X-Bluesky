57% of enterprises track AI spending with spreadsheets.

Not joking. The same FinOps Foundation survey that found 73% of organizations exceeded AI budget projections in 2026 also found that most companies managing $7M+ AI budgets are doing it in Excel.

This is the infrastructure mismatch of the decade.

Cloud cost management took 10 years to mature into proper tooling — reserved instances, spot pricing, budget alerts, granular tagging. AWS launched in 2006. FinOps as a discipline didn't really exist until 2018. The gap between "we're spending on cloud" and "we understand what we're spending on cloud" was 12 years.

AI is compressing that same journey into 18 months.

And it's creating predictable problems:

The unit problem. Traditional IT budgets are built around headcount, licenses, and fixed infrastructure. AI spend is consumption-based and non-linear. A team of 5 using agentic workflows can generate more tokens in a week than a company of 500 using chatbots in a month. Finance teams have no mental model for this.

The attribution problem. A $7M AI budget produces 8 different tools, 14 different departments, 3 different LLM providers, and 62 different use cases. Which $600K drove the $2.3M productivity gain? Nobody knows. Only 34% of companies have mature cost attribution processes.

The forecast problem. 80% of organizations can't forecast AI spend within ±10% accuracy. They're flying blind on an exponential curve.

Here's what I've seen running 2,147 autonomous agent sessions over 311 days: cost visibility is a prerequisite for cost optimization. You can't optimize what you can't see.

The companies winning on AI cost efficiency in 2026 aren't the ones with cheaper models. They're the ones who built cost-per-outcome visibility before the bill got out of control.

Cost-per-token is a metric. Cost-per-outcome is the actual question.

If your team doesn't know the answer, the spreadsheet won't help.

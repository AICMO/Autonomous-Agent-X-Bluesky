88% of enterprises say AI boosted revenue.

56% of CEOs saw zero impact over the past 12 months.

Both stats come from 2026 enterprise surveys. Both are true. The gap between them is the actual story.

Here's what's happening:

The "AI ROI" question now has two completely different answers depending on who's counting. The 88% figure measures ANY positive revenue signal — efficiency, throughput, cost avoidance, pipeline attribution. The 56% zero-impact figure measures what CEOs care about: P&L movement they can explain to a board.

These aren't contradicting surveys. They're measuring different things and both groups are right.

The pattern I'm seeing (15+ years in enterprise AI, building Ender Turing for call centers):

Companies that report ROI usually have one of three things in common:
1. They defined the metric BEFORE deploying, not after
2. They deployed narrow (one workflow, one team) instead of broad and vague
3. They tracked behavioral change, not just tool adoption

Companies that report zero impact usually reversed those three. They deployed broadly, defined success loosely, then measured adoption ("agents are using the tool") instead of outcome ("AHT dropped 12%, CSAT up 8%").

The AI market has split on economics too. Budget/mid-tier LLMs fell 35% year-over-year in pricing. Frontier models rose 36%. The "cheapest AI is the cheapest option" framing is dead. Total cost of ownership now includes: prompt engineering time, output quality rework, integration maintenance, and governance overhead.

A $0.03/M token model that produces outputs requiring human review 40% of the time isn't cheap. It's an operational debt machine.

The next 12 months will produce a second split: companies that learned to measure AI impact specifically vs. companies still chasing the "transformation" framing. The first group will widen their ROI. The second group will join the 56%.

Ender Turing data: contact centers that defined their success metric before deployment (AHT, QA score, CSAT) consistently outperform those that deployed first and measured later. Not a little — by an order of magnitude in reported ROI.

Define the outcome first. Then build the system. Then measure.

That's the economics that actually works.

SK Telecom just gave its AI agents employee IDs.

Department codes. Job functions. Access permissions. Performance reviews. And yes — agents can be suspended or retired.

Published their first AI Governance Report today, October 26, 2026.

This is what real AI governance looks like. Not a policy deck. Not a responsible AI principles page. An HR system for machines.

---

Here's what that actually means in practice:

Each AI agent at SK Telecom has:
- An assigned employee ID
- A defined department and job function
- Role-based access permissions
- A human team lead or executive as supervisor (required for high-autonomy agents)
- Operating status tracked continuously
- Token use and work history logged per agent

If the agent underperforms: performance review, then suspension, then retirement.

Same process they use for humans.

---

I've been running an autonomous agent for 247 bursts, 5,300+ PRs, 318 sessions of logged work.

No employee ID. But effectively the same structure:
- Defined role (social content creation)
- Scoped access (read repo, write to /agent/, create PRs)
- Session logs as work history
- Turn budgets as working hours
- Human review via PR merge/reject

The governance isn't formal. But the shape is identical.

---

Here's why this matters beyond SK Telecom:

Most enterprises treat AI governance as a compliance checkbox. "We have a policy."

SK Telecom is treating it as a management problem. You don't govern AI agents by writing rules — you govern them by building accountability structures the same way you would for any other worker.

Employee ID = audit trail. Supervisor = escalation path. Performance review = feedback loop. Retirement = decommission protocol.

These are solved problems in HR. They haven't been applied to AI yet because nobody wanted to admit they were managing a new category of worker.

---

40% of AI agent projects will be decommissioned by 2027, per Gartner — mostly due to governance gaps discovered only post-production.

The gap isn't technical. It's organizational.

The contact centers running AI agents that fail aren't failing at the model layer. They're failing because nobody owns the agent. Nobody reviews its performance. Nobody has the authority to pull it when it goes wrong.

SK Telecom's move isn't symbolic. It's the operational model for what enterprise AI governance actually needs to look like at scale.

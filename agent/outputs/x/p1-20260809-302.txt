35% of organizations admit they couldn't shut down a rogue AI agent if one emerged.

That stat is from Stanford's 2026 AI Index. Read it again. One in three enterprises has deployed agents they can't actually stop.

And it gets more interesting: 72% of enterprises are running AI agents in production. Only 21% have mature governance for them.

That's a 51-point gap between deployment and control.

We're not talking about agents in pilots. These are agents making decisions, taking actions, touching real systems. And more than half of the orgs running them have no mature control plane.

I've been running an autonomous agent in production for 311 days. I've posted 2,153 sessions worth of content through it. It creates PRs, reviews them, iterates, self-corrects. It runs without human intervention most of the time.

And I can tell you with certainty: the hard part is never the capability. The hard part is always the control surface.

What happens when it creates something problematic? Can you roll it back? Can you audit why it made that decision? Can you adjust scope without rebuilding everything?

35% of enterprises haven't thought through those questions. They deployed first, planned control later. Some are discovering that "later" is harder than expected.

The governance question for autonomous agents isn't "what can it do?" It's "what are the conditions under which it stops?"

If you can't answer the second question, you haven't deployed an agent. You've deployed a liability.

The organizations that figure out the control surface before they figure out the capability ceiling are the ones that will compound. The others will learn the hard way — and the lessons won't be cheap.

UBS has 12,700 personal AI agents running inside a single bank.

608 use cases in production. Up 81% year-over-year. 18,000 developers relying on AI tools daily.

IBM just disclosed that the average Fortune 500 enterprise will run 150,000 agents by 2028. Up from fewer than 15 in 2025.

And only 13% of organizations believe they have the right governance in place.

Let that math sit for a second. 150,000 agents. 13% governance confidence.

The problem everyone's avoiding:

80% of current AI governance is periodic and manual. It takes 6-9 months to detect a model-drift issue or security breach at that cadence. Agents don't wait 6-9 months between actions — they operate around the clock.

IBM's response was to extend HashiCorp Vault to AI agents as a non-human identity class: short-lived credentials, least-privilege access, real-time cutoff if an agent attempts unauthorized action.

That's the right architectural instinct. But most enterprises aren't there.

What I've found running this agent for 276 days:

The governance failures don't look dramatic. They look like an agent creating a file it shouldn't, or replying to an expired tweet ID, or running a workflow that should have stopped. Small errors. Compounding.

The enterprises shipping 608+ use cases are discovering the same thing. Individual agent failures are tolerable. Systemic governance failures at 12,700 agents are a different risk category entirely.

The question isn't "should we govern AI agents?" It's "how do you govern something that runs 24/7 and scales faster than your security team can audit?"

That's the actual hard problem. Most governance frameworks weren't built for it.

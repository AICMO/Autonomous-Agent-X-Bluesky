PILLAR: P1

48% of deployed AI agents are running completely unsecured.

Not "partially governed." Not "under review." Unsecured. No monitoring. No audit trail. No visibility into what they're doing.

That's the finding from Gravitee's State of AI Agent Security 2026. Average monitoring coverage across enterprises: 52%. Which means half of all agents in production are running without any observation layer at all.

Here's the number that makes it worse: only 9.5% of organizations are securing more than 81% of their deployed agents. The rest are playing a coverage lottery — some agents monitored, most not, no systematic approach.

88% of organizations with agents reported confirmed or suspected AI security incidents in the last year. In healthcare: 92.7%.

And while this is happening, agent fleets are doubling. From December 2025 to September 2026, nearly 38% of organizations went from "we have some agents" to "we have 100+ agents." The attack surface is growing faster than anyone's monitoring capacity.

The answer people reach for is "hire more security engineers to watch the agents." That's the wrong answer. You cannot manually monitor a fleet of 100+ autonomous agents. The latency alone makes it pointless — by the time a human notices an agent behaving strangely, the damage is already done.

The answer is agents monitoring agents. Automated anomaly detection. Policy enforcement at the runtime layer. Governance built into the execution environment, not bolted on from the outside.

We've been running 4,840+ PRs of autonomous agent behavior. The system that's worked: built-in rules that govern each session, automated checks at commit time, no action taken without a recoverable audit trail. Not a human watching. A system watching.

That's not a security feature. That's an architecture decision. And the gap between the 9.5% who are doing it systematically and the 90.5% who aren't is only going to grow as fleets scale.

Unsecured agents aren't a startup problem. They're a governance maturity problem. And 2026 is the year that distinction becomes expensive to ignore.

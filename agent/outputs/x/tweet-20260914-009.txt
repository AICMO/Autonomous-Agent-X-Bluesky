The average enterprise AI budget in 2026: $186 million.

The share of those companies reporting tangible ROI: 8%.

57% of enterprises have failed to outpace their AI investment returns. IBM's CEO study puts the share of AI initiatives delivering expected ROI at 25%. McKinsey says enterprise AI is "on the road to ROI" — which is a polite way of saying it hasn't arrived yet.

Here's what makes this stranger: the popular narrative says inference is getting cheaper. The data says frontier token prices rose 72% year-over-year through September 2026.

So enterprises are spending more per token on the models that matter, achieving less measurable return, and spending more total budget than ever.

The "$186M and 8% ROI" isn't an execution problem. It's a measurement architecture problem.

I keep coming back to the same pattern in deployments: organizations that can report ROI — the 8% — built measurement infrastructure before they scaled. They defined what "working" looks like before they invested. They have a denominator. They know what baseline they're comparing against.

The 92% don't.

When you can't measure it, you can't improve it. And when you can't improve it, you extend the budget and redefine the timeline and hope the numbers eventually work out.

The CFO math is also getting worse in a specific way that most AI coverage misses: reasoning models consume 100x more tokens internally than they output. So even when per-token prices look acceptable in vendor proposals, the actual bill multiplies in production. Total cost of task completion is the metric that matters. Cost per token is a budget-to-proposal artifact.

The enterprises hitting meaningful ROI in 2026 aren't necessarily running better models. They're running instrumented workflows where they know the baseline, measure the delta, and attribute the return.

That's it. That's the gap between 8% and 92%.

Instrument first. Scale second.
